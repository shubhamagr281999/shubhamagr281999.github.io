Robotic charging challenge: reachability-driven arm design

A robot arm that plugs a charger into a car nobody parked carefully. I scored every cell of the arm's task space by how many distinct orientations it could reach that cell in, then used that field to choose link lengths. Reaching the port's position is useless if you cannot arrive along the plug's axis.

Reachability, not reach

The usual way to answer "can the arm get there" is a binary reachable volume. For a docking problem that is the wrong question. A charging plug has to arrive along the port's axis, so a cell the arm can only enter in one awkward orientation is functionally unreachable even though a reach map marks it green.

So the sweep records the full end-effector pose, position and approach axis, for every collision-free configuration, and the output is a count per task-space cell of how many distinct orientations the arm can achieve there. That field is what you actually design the mechanism against, and it is what made the link-length change a measurement rather than an opinion.

Making the sweep fast enough to iterate with

A four-dimensional joint grid with a full pairwise collision check at every node is expensive, and the first version was exactly that. It was also, briefly, worse than that: an early version had a live plot call and a one-second pause inside the innermost loop, because I was watching it to debug the collision boxes.

The fix that mattered was hoisting the collision check into every level of the nested sweep. If link two's box already collides at a given angle, the entire subtree of joint angles below it is never entered. Same idea as branch and bound. That pruning is what bought the resolution: the final field is sampled at 0.08 radians, which the naive version could not have reached in the time available.

There is also a bug worth owning. In the first version I built the sweep ranges for three joints from the second joint's limits, a copy-paste that silently swept about half the real workspace. It is fixed in the production version. You only catch that class of error by plotting the field and noticing the shape is wrong, which is an argument for plotting things.

What was mine and what was not

This was a nine-person team over nine days, and the repository is upstream-owned by a teammate; mine is a fork.

Mine: the collision model, the workspace characterisation and the link-geometry iteration driven off it, the screw-axis conversions, the workspace plotting, the bridge from the MATLAB planner into ROS, and the Simscape and 3D-world integration.

Not mine: the analytic inverse kinematics with all its branches and singular cases, the recursive Newton-Euler torque computation, the trajectory generation and trapezoidal time-scaling, the per-trajectory energy figures, and the vision pipeline that finds the charge port.

One story from the team's half is worth telling because it is the best engineering moment in the repository and it is not mine. The velocity plan scales a trapezoidal profile in joint space, then checks the resulting link-frame speeds, and if any exceeds the safety limit near the car it shrinks the driving joint's peak velocity and re-plans, looping until the constraint holds. That retry loop is an honest confession that joint-space limits do not translate into a safe end-effector speed, which is the sort of thing you only discover by checking.
