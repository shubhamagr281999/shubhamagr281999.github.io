Aug 2022 – Jun 2023 · Master's thesis, IIT Bombay

Control and simulation of a soft continuum robot

A five-segment silicone robot I fabricated, closed-loop controlled from an overhead camera. My controller needed torque and the servos could not deliver it, so I cast a torque sensor from the same silicone as the robot. Then that failed too, and the fix was to change what the controller commands.

  • Control
  • Simulation

My part Sole author. Model validation and extension, the simulator, the controller, and the hardware I designed, cast and assembled.

Read the thesis (PDF, 1.5 MB)

Problem

Soft robots deform continuously, so their state is effectively infinite-dimensional. The models accurate enough to trust are too slow to sit inside a control loop, and the models fast enough to run in the loop are only valid for slow motion and specific shapes. I wanted one model that was both fast enough to close a loop and accurate enough to be worth closing.

Approach

I represented the planar body as a mass-spring network whose stiffness matrix is rebuilt from the deformed configuration every step, which is what captures geometric nonlinearity at large deflection without paying finite-element cost. I validated it against analytical solutions, a chained beam-constraint model and FEM, then built a predictive controller on it that forms a numerical Jacobian by running five perturbed copies of the simulator in parallel, each snapshotting state, perturbing one input, predicting forward and rewinding. The robot is five cast silicone segments with rigid printed ends, tendon-driven, tracked by markers through an overhead 4K camera.

Outcome

Set-point accuracy of 0.3 mm at the tip, with trajectory tracking typically between 0.6 and 0.8 mm, against a vision system that itself resolves pose to 0.35 mm and slope to 0.005 radians. The model matches FEM to within a few percent on tip deflection across aspect ratios, and its first three natural frequencies sit within about 2 percent. The control loop runs at 2 to 4 Hz, which is a pseudo-static controller's honest limit and the thing I would attack next. Awarded a departmental Research Award.

A model that is both fast and honest

The planar body is a square mesh. Every vertex is a node carrying a share of the mass, every edge and diagonal is a spring, and springs act in parallel where squares share an edge. The dynamics are the usual mass, damping and stiffness form with Rayleigh damping.

The load-bearing detail is that the stiffness matrix is rebuilt from the deformed configuration at every step. That is what lets a lumped mass-spring network capture geometric nonlinearity at large deflection, and it is the whole reason this competes with finite elements for accuracy while staying cheap enough to run repeatedly.

I did not invent the mass-spring topology. It comes from my supervisor's earlier work; my contribution was validating it in the large-deformation range and building the first control stack on it. I benchmarked it four independent ways: against linear analytical solutions in tension and compression, against a chained beam-constraint model under end force and pure moment, against FEM for distributed bending across three aspect ratios, and against FEM for the first three natural frequencies.

One benchmark disagrees. The step-response settling value in one axis does not match FEM, by about a millimetre. I reported it rather than dropping the plot.

A Jacobian from seven simulators

The controller is a deliberately simplified predictive controller, and the thesis says so plainly rather than calling it MPC and hoping. At each step it needs the sensitivity of tip position to each of five section torques.

Rather than linearise a nonlinear network with thousands of degrees of freedom analytically, I got the Jacobian by brute parallelism. Seven simulator processes run at once: one plant, one unperturbed predictor, and five step-response predictors. Each predictor advances with the plant, snapshots the full state, applies a perturbation to exactly one input, predicts a second ahead on a coarser timestep, publishes the resulting tip pose, and then rewinds to the snapshot. A compiler node assembles the five differences into the matrix and divides by the step height. All of it inside one control period.

The control increment is then the minimum-norm solution through a pseudo-inverse, saturated on its largest entry with the rest scaled by the same ratio, so the commanded direction is preserved rather than rotated by per-entry clipping.

That pattern, snapshot, perturb, roll out, rewind, is the same shape as a batched rollout worker in a model-based learning setup, which is most of why this project still feels relevant to what I want to do next.

One methodological choice worth stating: the controller's predictor runs a coarser mesh than the plant it controls. The controller is therefore always somewhat wrong about the plant by construction, so the simulation result means something instead of validating itself.

The sensor I built and had to abandon

The controller is formulated in torque. The servos' gear ratio meant torque could not be commanded reliably, only position. So I built the missing sensor: a torsional torque sensor cast from the same silicone as the segments, in a two-part printed mould I designed, with rigid load-transfer ends. I swept its length and diameter against maximum shear stress to trade range against resolution, built several, and characterised each by hanging known weights while an encoder read deflection, getting a clean linear fit over about plus or minus 120 degrees.

Then it failed three ways at once. The range of torque it could transfer was too small to be useful. Optimising the geometry to widen that range sacrificed the resolution the robot needed, and there was no geometry that satisfied both. And because the sensing element was silicone, its properties drifted over time and its fatigue life was poor. I had built a drift-prone sensor to measure a drift-prone plant.

The fix was not a better sensor. While characterising it I had noticed that slope and torque are close to linearly related over the working range. So I changed what the controller commands: the high-level controller still reasons in torque, but its output passes through a torque-to-slope conversion, a low-level slope loop drives the servo position, and the measured slope is pushed back through a slope-to-torque conversion so the predictive model upstream still sees the quantity it needs to simulate forward.

Two conversions bracketing a new inner loop, and the controller never learns that its actuator changed.

The material would not hold still

This one is not in the thesis. It is in the commit log, which is arguably where the honest version of a project lives.

Over roughly ten days in April I went from "the Young's modulus should be near 1.8 MPa" to "found the correct value to be around 1.62 MPa" to a commit that reads, in capitals partway through, that there was a significant change in material property between two identical experiments run twelve days apart, and that it needed investigating. The model in the final thesis uses about 1.54 MPa, while every clean beam benchmark earlier in the document uses 1 MPa.

Cast silicone kept curing and its stiffness drifted under me while I was trying to identify it. I ended up characterising hysteresis deliberately, with slowed motion and dwell time at the extremes, to separate that from the drift.

It cost the better part of two weeks and it is the single most useful thing I learned on the project: a parameter you identified last fortnight is not a constant, and a sim-to-real gap is often a calibration that expired rather than a modelling error.

What the numbers are, precisely

Tip set-point control lands at 0.3 mm. Trajectory tracking averages under a millimetre when slow, with the four measured runs coming in at 0.8, 2.2, 0.6 and 0.6 mm; accuracy degrades with speed and is worst at sharp direction changes, recovering to within 0.3 mm once past the corner.

Those numbers sit against a vision system that resolves pose to 0.35 mm and slope to 0.005 radians, which is the floor for all of it. The low-level slope loop is accurate to that 0.005 radian limit.

The loop runs at 2 to 4 Hz with a rise time around a second and a closed-loop bandwidth near 0.25 Hz. That is the honest ceiling of a pseudo-static controller that does not account for the system's dynamics, and it is what I would go after next: there is a half-finished controller in the repository that accumulates impulse-response matrices and distributes a minimum-energy input across the horizon instead, which is the right attack on exactly this limitation.
