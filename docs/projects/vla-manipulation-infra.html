Robot-learning models and infrastructure for VLA manipulation

Six months from fine-tuning open-source VLA models to designing our own. The shift needed three things: a model designed around an open-source VLM, a workflow that turns mismatched open-source robot datasets into one trustworthy training set, and the controllers and evaluation that run the resulting policies on real arms.

  • Physical AI
  • Simulation
  • Control

My part Part of the design group for our in-house VLA model. Designed and built the cross-embodiment dataset-validation workflow. Created the simulation and evaluation framework, sole-authored the dataset curation tool, and ran the action-space experiments.

Problem

Open-source VLA models and robot datasets exist, but they do not fit together on their own. Every public dataset records a different robot with its own joint order, frame conventions, gripper polarity and quirks, and a training set that mixes them wrongly fails silently. The policy's action space has to be chosen deliberately. And a policy that outputs end-effector motion needs a controller that turns it into safe joint commands on real hardware.

Approach

I work across the whole path. On the model, I fine-tuned open-source VLAs on our data and am now part of the group designing our own around an open-source VLM backbone: the action head, the projectors, and how information about the robot enters the model. On the data, I designed a validation workflow that maps each open-source dataset onto a verified model of its robot and proves the mapping before any of it reaches training, alongside a curation tool for our own teleoperation data. On deployment, I ran controlled action-space experiments with π₀.₅, built a constrained IK controller for end-effector actions, and created the framework that evaluates a policy in MuJoCo or on real arms through one code path.

Outcome

Switching π₀.₅ to delta-joint actions raised task completion by 30% over absolute-joint actions, trained on mixed simulation and real data and evaluated in both. The validation workflow has taken five open-source datasets across six robot embodiments through a full-dataset sweep. The IK controller cut end-effector tracking error in simulation from about 2.5 cm to 0.29 mm, solving in about a millisecond per arm with full collision checking.

Designing our own model

I am part of the design group for an in-house VLA. It is built around an open-source vision-language model as the backbone, and the design work is everything around it: the action head, the projectors between modalities, and how information about the robot itself enters the model. The details are proprietary.

What I bring is time spent fine-tuning existing models on our own data. Seeing where they forget, how they react to a data mix, and which action representations they learn well is what our design choices start from.

What fine-tuning open models taught me

Before designing anything, I fine-tuned open-source VLAs, π₀.₅ and OpenVLA-OFT, on our own bimanual teleoperation data and wrote the team's training runbook. Three lessons from that work carried forward.

Sequential fine-tuning forgets. Continuing from an earlier checkpoint on new tasks erodes what the model already did well, so I ran it as a controlled comparison against fine-tuning the same data from the base model, and a new action space always starts again from the base.

The data mix is a training decision. Tasks, operators and simulation all arrive in different volumes, and the dataset format has no sampling weights. So the curation tool I wrote rebalances the mix by repeating or thinning episodes per task, and the experiments compared mixed simulation and real data against real data alone.

Normalisation fails silently. Fine-tuning on new episodes while keeping the original dataset's statistics means out-of-range values normalise outside the range the model was trained on, and nothing downstream clips or complains. I built a guard for it into the curation tool: it checks the current selection against reference statistics and names the bound it used in every message, because the same data flags none of a hundred episodes under one bound and all hundred under another.

Making open-source robot datasets safe to train on

A clean dataset is one of the cheapest wins in training quality, and public robot datasets are each unclean in their own way. Every one records a different robot, with its own joint order, coordinate frames, gripper convention and camera layout, and the dataset card is often wrong about some of it. Mix them naively and the training run fails silently.

I designed and built a workflow that settles all of this before ingestion. Each robot is first converted into a verified MuJoCo model under one shared frame contract: named sites for the base, the cameras and each gripper, with camera sites in the OpenCV convention so a site's pose is the camera extrinsic. Each dataset then goes through six phases and five human gates. Every claim from the paper or the loader code is treated as a hypothesis, and forward kinematics decides. The output is a machine-readable mapping (joint map, frames, gripper polarity for both state and command, cameras and exclusions), so ingestion knows exactly what to read and how to transform it into one training representation.

Two checks earned their place the hard way. Frame candidates are ranked on position and rotation together, because on one dataset a wrong quaternion order still fitted position to 2.5 mm while the rotation was off by 51 degrees. And every check carries a sensitivity test that perturbs link lengths by a centimetre and joint zeros by two degrees in memory, to prove it would catch a real error. The final sweep replays the fitted mapping on every episode of the full release, streamed from cloud storage under a hard disk budget and resumable. That step exists because an earlier sample-only check let thousands of malformed episodes through.

A coding agent executes the workflow from playbooks I wrote, and every gate needs my decision. Two teammates now use it for new datasets.

Choosing what the policy outputs

The action representation turned out to matter more than most of the training recipe. I set up the comparisons as controlled experiments in π₀.₅: absolute joint positions against delta-joint actions and end-effector deltas. Each new configuration was added alongside the old ones, so every earlier run stays reproducible.

Delta-joint actions, trained on mixed simulation and real data, raised task completion by 30% over absolute-joint actions, evaluated both in simulation and on the real arms. For the end-effector action space I started again from the base model, because with a different action space there is no reason to expect the old action-expert weights to transfer.

Before training I checked the data semantics numerically and found two silent issues upstream. The training code never reads the padding flags, so the last steps of each episode train on a clamped tail as if it were real motion. And the end-effector configuration shares one normalisation range across the whole action horizon, which stretches tiny per-step deltas to fill it.

Getting end-effector actions onto real arms

An end-effector policy is no use on hardware without a controller that turns its output into safe joint commands. The servos take joint positions, and the stop-gap this replaced, a damped-least-squares IK inside the policy, had no joint limits, no collision awareness, and plateaued at about 2.5 cm of error.

I built a differential-IK controller that solves a small quadratic program every tick, with joint position limits, velocity limits and collision avoidance as hard constraints. I configured an existing solver, mink, instead of writing one, because it reads MuJoCo models natively: the simulation's own scene and collision geometry become the solver's model, so simulation and the robot computer run the same code. The two arms are solved one after the other in a shared world, so the second arm treats the first arm's freshly commanded pose as an obstacle. Each solve is seeded from the observed joints, so the controller cannot drift away from the real arm.

In a simulation benchmark it tracks to 0.29 mm and solves in about 1.2 ms per arm against 1,728 collision pairs. The live timing tail set the control rate: at 200 Hz too many ticks ran over budget, so it ships at 100 Hz, chosen from the 99th percentile and not the mean.

Making a slow model safe to control with

Policy inference is far slower than a control tick. The obvious structure, infer then act, either stalls the controller or drags it down to the model's rate. Neither is acceptable on an arm.

The runner instead uses four wall-clock-paced threads that exchange latest values and never block on a slower peer. The backend steps physics and renders cameras into an immutable image bundle that is swapped without locking. Inference runs asynchronously and its result is drained on a later tick. A missing action chunk means hold the last setpoint rather than emit a discontinuity.

The result is the design goal: a slow model costs smoothness, not correctness.
